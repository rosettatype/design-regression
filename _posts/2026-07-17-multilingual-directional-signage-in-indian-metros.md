## Introduction

### Background to the study

This article examines the integration of Devanagari within multilingual and multi-script directional signage across India’s metro networks. The analysis presented here builds upon my [previous work,](#sn:sn1) which explored the challenges of adapting Latin-script wayfinding principles to Devanagari in India’s multilingual public transport systems. The current study highlights diverse approaches to script prioritisation, typographic harmonisation, and spatial organisation across nine operational metro systems: Pune, Nagpur, Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Mumbai, Kolkata, Ahmedabad, Lucknow, and Delhi.

Selected from India’s 17 operational networks, these nine metro systems offer a broad overview of multilingual signage whilst focusing on those prominently using Devanagari in primary wayfinding. This focus excludes systems with minimal Devanagari, such as Chennai (mostly Tamil/English) and Kochi (infrequent Hindi). The selection includes Lucknow, located within Uttar Pradesh (UP), and Delhi, whose metro network extends significantly into the National Capital Region, serving areas in UP (like Noida) as well as Haryana (like Gurgaon). With UP thus represented by Lucknow and the Delhi system’s Noida connection, an analysis of other state systems like Agra and Kanpur was deferred for future study. In contrast, Bengaluru was included specifically to illustrate the impact of language policy, given its removal of Devanagari. The remaining systems (Pune, Nagpur, Hyderabad, [Mumbai,](#sn:sn2) [Kolkata,](#sn:sn3) Ahmedabad) were selected as they fit within the scope of the study, sufficient visual data was available, and they provide geographical and linguistic diversity.

### Context and significance

The rapid expansion of metro systems in India’s urban centres has necessitated multilingual and multi-script signage that consistently integrates English (Latin script), a regional language (using scripts like Devanagari, Bengali, or Telugu), and, in most cases, Hindi (Devanagari) to serve its diverse populations. However, the absence of standardised guidelines has led to inconsistent design practices, as observed across the nine metro systems analysed in this study. Each metro network demonstrates distinct approaches to linguistic hierarchy, typography, and spatial organisation. By creating fragmented and unpredictable visual environments, these inconsistencies risk increasing the cognitive load on passengers, which can lead to confusion and hinder efficient wayfinding.

### Research goals and methods

This study analyses the integration of Devanagari within multilingual wayfinding systems across nine Indian metro networks. It examines variations in script prioritisation, typographic elements (such as typeface selection and text alignment), and visual coherence, evaluating their consistency with established design principles for transportation systems.

A primary component of this analysis involved a collection of visual data gathered from [multiple sources.](#sn:sn4) While direct, in-person observations were not possible for most of the networks studied, this combined dataset provided a diverse collection of images sufficient for examining the signage design.

The following section presents detailed descriptions of each system’s directional signage, followed by a comparative summary of key characteristics.

## Descriptive analysis of the metro system directional signage

### 1. Pune

Pune Metro, operational since 2022, serves the cities of Pune and Pimpri-Chinchwad, one of Maharashtra’s major and rapidly growing urban centres. Its directional signage incorporates three languages: Marathi (in Devanagari), Hindi (in Devanagari), and English (in Latin script). Marathi, the regional language, is included to reflect the area’s linguistic identity. The inclusion of Hindi caters to the Hindi-speaking immigrant population, while English accommodates non-Hindi speakers and international [visitors.](#sn:sn5)

### 2. Nagpur

Nagpur Metro, operational since 2019, uses a multilingual approach similar to Pune with signs in Marathi (in Devanagari), Hindi (in  Devanagari), and English (in Latin script).

### 3. Bengaluru (Namma Metro)

Namma Metro initially implemented a trilingual signage system incorporating Kannada (in Kannada script), Hindi (in Devanagari), and English (in Latin script). Prior to the removal of Hindi, the typographic treatment of Devanagari varied depending on the sign’s deployment. While large station indicators utilised a variant of the legacy Yogesh typeface, the internal directional signage relied on Mangal, a standard system font. Following local opposition to perceived Hindi imposition, the current system retains only Kannada and English, eliminating Devanagari entirely ([Times of India,
2017](#ref:times-2017)).

### 4. Hyderabad

Hyderabad Metro integrates four languages and scripts in its signage: Telugu (in Telugu script), English (in Latin script), Hindi (in Devanagari), and Urdu (in Arabic [script)](#sn:sn6). Telugu and English are prioritised at the top of signage panels with larger font sizes reflecting Hyderabad’s status as a technology hub and its Telugu-speaking majority. Text alignment varies by script: Telugu and Hindi are right-aligned, while English and, notably, Urdu are left-aligned, which is unconventional for the typically right-to-left Arabic script. Thick red and blue lines separate language pairs, with thinner white lines distinguishing destination information.

### 5. Mumbai

The wayfinding system of Mumbai Metro Line 1 uses Marathi (Devanagari), Hindi (Devanagari), and English (Latin script). Marathi and Hindi receive equal visual priority through identical heavy font weight and size, separated by a thick red line. English appears below in a lighter font weight, indicating secondary status. However, this hierarchy is contradicted when ‘EXIT’ appears in all capitals, as capitalisation signals importance despite the lighter weight and subordinate placement. On exit gate signs, essential information including gate numbers and locations appears only in English, excluding Marathi and Hindi users. Linguistic disparity extends to station branding, where sponsor prefixes such as ‘HDFC SKY’ at Ghatkopar use only Latin script. These sponsor names, rendered in all capitals, visually dominate the station names. Text alignment varies between left-aligned and apparently arbitrary positions, creating spatial disorganisation without a predictable reading start point. One efficiency is writing identical words like ‘घाटकोपर’ (Ghatkopar) once rather than duplicating them for Marathi and Hindi.

### 6. Kolkata

Kolkata Metro, India’s oldest, uses a trilingual wayfinding system incorporating Bengali (Bengali script), Hindi (Devanagari), and English (Latin). The legacy signage divides information into distinct rectangular panels, with all text consistently left-aligned within its respective compartment. In the upper sections, Bengali text is stacked directly above Hindi text. English text is positioned separately in a lower section. Rendered entirely in capital letters, the English text appears in a larger size and heavier font weight compared to the regional scripts. Furthermore, supplementary details, such as the words ‘Platform No.’ in all three languages, are presented in a significantly smaller font size and stacked vertically next to the platform numerals.

### 7. Ahmedabad

Ahmedabad Metro, serving Ahmedabad and Gandhinagar, uses a trilingual system incorporating Gujarati (in Gujarati script), Hindi (Devanagari), and English (Latin). The wayfinding system was designed by the National Institute of Design (NID), one of India’s premier design institutions ([Metro Rail
News, 2017](#ref:metro-rail-2017)). A ‘No Exit’ sign was observed where the vertical spacing between the scripts is unequal. The Gujarati and Hindi text are placed closely together, while a larger space is left below the English text.

### 8. Lucknow

Lucknow Metro employs a bilingual wayfinding system with Hindi (in Devanagari) and English (in Latin script), and unlike Mumbai Metro, it provides supplementary information in both languages on its signs.

### 9. Delhi

Delhi Metro, India’s largest and most comprehensive metro system, uses a bilingual wayfinding system with Hindi (in Devanagari) and English (in Latin script). This approach caters for both the local Hindi-speaking population and the city’s English-speaking residents and visitors.

## Conclusion

The analysis of nine Indian metro systems provides critical insights into the integration of Devanagari within multilingual and multi-script wayfinding signage. Three overarching themes emerge:

### Linguistic diversity and script harmonisation

All systems employ multilingual signage comprising two to four languages, reflecting India’s linguistic plurality. While Devanagari (used for Hindi and regional languages) coexists alongside the Latin, Bengali, Telugu, and Arabic scripts, its visual harmonisation with these different writing systems remains inconsistent. For example, Mumbai Metro’s singular representation of identical Marathi and Hindi words (see <a href="#fig:f10">Figure 10</a>) efficiently reduces visual clutter. Conversely, Kolkata Metro’s strictly compartmentalised layout (see <a href="#fig:f11">Figure 11</a>) creates a fragmented reading experience that undermines overall readability.

### Hierarchical tensions and cultural dynamics

Systems prioritise scripts differently: regional languages (e.g., Marathi in Pune, see <a href="#fig:f1">Figure 1</a>) often dominate through size and positioning, while others (e.g., Delhi, see <a href="#fig:f16">Figure 16</a>) grant equal status to Hindi and English. Political influences further shape these hierarchies, as seen in Bengaluru’s removal of Hindi. These variations reflect unresolved tensions between regional identity, national policies, and global accessibility.

### Typographic inconsistencies

The practical application of typography reveals numerous inconsistencies:

**Typefaces:** Typeface selection reveals a fundamental split between reliance on legacy system fonts and the adoption of modern typographic solutions. The majority of metros fall into the former category, primarily using variants of Yogesh (often distributed as part of the Shree-Lipi software suite). This pre-Unicode, legacy Devanagari typeface from the 1980s and 1990s became heavily ingrained in local desktop publishing and has subsequently been widely adopted for public signage. Bengaluru also followed this approach, using the common system font Mangal for its now-removed Hindi text. In contrast, a few systems use contemporary fonts through two distinct strategies. Nagpur achieves consistency with the unified Mukta typeface for all languages, while Ahmedabad mixes modern fonts for each script: Kohinoor (Devanagari), Mukta (Gujarati), and Hind (Latin). The coexistence of these legacy, unified, and mixed-font strategies underscores a profound lack of standardisation.

**Alignment and spatial organisation:** A range of alignment strategies is observed across the systems. The most common approach is direction-based alignment, where text is justified in the same direction the sign indicates, as seen in Pune, Nagpur, Bengaluru, Lucknow, and Delhi. Other networks apply alternative rules, such as Ahmedabad’s opposite direction alignment and Hyderabad’s script-specific alignment. In contrast, both Mumbai and Kolkata feature inconsistent layouts that vary between signs without a discernible system. Beyond alignment, the inconsistent application of differentiators such as spacing and coloured lines often creates additional visual noise, as observed in Hyderabad.

These inconsistencies highlight the absence of standardised guidelines for Devanagari-centric multilingual typography. While the script’s structural complexity demands tailored solutions, ad-hoc decisions driven by local pragmatism or politics result in fragmented user experiences. Skilful typography and clear hierarchical structuring can address this issue. Future efforts must prioritise empirical legibility testing, further exploration and development of script-specific typefaces, and flexible frameworks that balance regional preferences with universal accessibility. As India’s urban mobility networks expand, such harmonisation will be critical to ensuring equitable navigation in linguistically diverse spaces.
